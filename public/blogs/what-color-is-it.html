Why Nebulae Look the Way They Do

People ask me this a lot, usually after seeing one of my nebula photos. "Are those colors real?" It's a fair question, and the honest answer is: it depends on what you mean by real.

Here's the short version. Nebulae are clouds of gas and dust scattered across space, and different gases glow in different colors when energized by nearby stars. Hydrogen tends to glow red. Oxygen glows blue-green. Sulfur produces a deep red, slightly different from hydrogen. These aren't arbitrary — each gas emits light at a specific wavelength, as reliable as a fingerprint. If you could float inside a nebula with your eyes fully adjusted, you'd see color. Just not necessarily the colors in my photos.

Where It Gets Interesting

To capture narrowband images, astrophotographers use specialized filters that block out almost everything except the specific wavelength emitted by one gas at a time. We're talking about hydrogen-alpha (Ha), oxygen-III (OIII), and sulfur-II (SII). Each filter isolates one signal. You end up with three separate grayscale images — one for each gas — and then the real decisions begin.

How you combine those three channels determines what your final image looks like. And this is where astrophotography starts to straddle the line between science and art.

The Hubble Palette

Many of NASA's most recognizable Hubble Space Telescope images — the ones that end up as desktop wallpapers and magazine covers — use what's called the SHO palette. The name tells you the assignment: Sulfur gets mapped to red, Hydrogen to green, and Oxygen to blue. This is a false-color technique, meaning the colors in the final image don't represent what your eye would see. They represent which gas is where. The famous "Pillars of Creation" image uses this palette. Those golden-orange columns aren't golden-orange in any literal sense — that's sulfur, rendered in a color our eyes can distinguish and our brains find striking.

Other Palettes

SHO is just one option. HOO maps hydrogen to red and oxygen to both green and blue, producing images that look closer to what a human eye might perceive — warmer, more natural-looking reds and blues. Some photographers use HOS or other channel arrangements depending on which gases dominate their target and what story they want the image to tell.

There's no governing body handing out rules here. Some astrophotographers treat palette choice as a scientific decision — trying to represent the data as faithfully as possible. Others treat it as an artistic one, choosing colors the way a painter chooses from a palette. Both approaches are legitimate. Both produce images that are, in their own way, accurate.

So Are the Colors "Real"?

The colors are real representations of real data. The gas is really there. The emissions are really happening. The color assignments are a choice — a translation from wavelengths our cameras can detect into colors our eyes can appreciate. Think of it like a map. A map isn't the territory, but a good map tells you something true about it.

When I process one of my nebula images, I'm making decisions at every step — which palette, how much to stretch the data, where to draw out faint detail and where to let the darkness stay dark. For my own images, I try to stick with the colors my telescope's sensor actually captures. Both of my scopes — a ZWO Seestar S30 and Seestar S50 — have a built-in narrowband filter I typically use for nebulae and an IRCUT filter for galaxies. If I've used a special palette, I'll say so.

The universe doesn't hand you a finished picture. You earn it one decision at a time.
